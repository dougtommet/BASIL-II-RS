Abstract (250 words)


Objectives: Delirium severity is an important measurement for clinical monitoring,

pathophysiologic studies, and treatment trials. We examined inter-rater reliability of delirium

severity ratings by trained clinical assessors using standardized protocols across different

clinical settings.

Design: Prospective, multisite observational study

Setting: Acute medical inpatient, elective surgical inpatient, and nursing home

Participants: Subsample (n=46) of participant cases for a reliability analysis conducted on 954

paired ratings by 7 clinical assessors across sites. The subsample was selected from older

adults (n=488; 46% delirium, 59% dementia) enrolled in the Better Assessment of Illness

(BASIL) II study.

Measurements: Clinical assessors were provided standardized summaries including cognitive

test results and delirium-specific assessments and used this information to rate delirium severity

on a 0-10 scale. Paired agreement was defined as delirium severity scores within 2 points. Inter-

rater reliability and variability were evaluated before and after a consensus meeting, utilizing a

modified Delphi approach to achieve agreement by prespecified criteria.

Results: Clinical assessors demonstrated high inter-rater agreement in delirium severity ratings

(weighted kappa, κ=0.84), which improved following consensus discussions (κ=0.93). Bland-

Altman analysis showed no evidence of systematic disagreement across the full range of

severity scores (linear trend for differences over average of two rater scores, b=0.03, 95%

confidence interval, -0.13, 0.20).

Conclusions: Trained clinical assessors demonstrated high inter-rater reliability in delirium

severity ratings across a reliability sample that included patients with dementia and a wide

range of delirium severity. These findings support feasibility of structured clinical assessment

INTRODUCTION

       Delirium is a common and serious medical syndrome in older adults. Among patients

with Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Dementias (ADRD), delirium occurs with greater

frequency, severity, and associated morbidity.1-4 Delirium severity, defined as the intensity or

burden of delirium features, has emerged as a clinically meaningful outcome for patient

monitoring, risk stratification, and evaluating response to treatment.5 Graded severity ratings

provide information beyond presence/absence indicators, enabling more precise tracking of

subthreshold symptoms, symptom fluctuation, and degrees of improvement over time.6,7

       Despite growing interest in delirium severity as an outcome, there is limited evidence on

the reliability of delirium severity ratings in research settings across diverse care environments,

and the reliability of such ratings has been questioned given their highly variable manifestations.

High quality studies rely on expert panel adjudication for reference standard delirium and related

diagnoses,7,8 but standardized clinical assessment protocols provide another opportunity for

research standardization that has not been well characterized. This is particularly important for

multisite research studies that depend on reproducible, high-quality data across sites.

       The Better Assessment of Illness (BASIL) II study is a multisite prospective cohort study

designed to develop and validate delirium severity instruments in older adults with and without

ADRD.9 As part of this effort, trained clinical assessors (CAs) conducted standardized delirium

assessments using structured cognitive and functional tools, including the Confusion

Assessment Method (CAM),10 Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA),11 and informant

interviews, across three care settings: acute medical, elective surgical, and post-acute nursing

home. CAs were clinicians and experienced clinical researchers and included at least one

delirium or dementia expert at each site. Our goal in this report was to assess overall agreement

of delirium severity rating among CAs, explore the effect of consensus discussion on inter-rater

reliability, and examine whether agreement varied by level of delirium severity and the presence

of dementia.

METHODS

Study Design and Procedures

       Study Sample. This study was conducted within the Better Assessment of Illness

(BASIL) II study, an NIH-funded prospective cohort study specifically designed to develop and

validate new delirium severity instruments for persons with and without dementia. An important

part of the data collection for the BASIL II study was completed by a team of 7 clinical assessors

across 3 study sites. The present analysis was conducted to evaluate the reliability of data

collection by the clinical assessors, which was essential to assure the validity of the overall

BASIL II study. Participants were enrolled from three clinical settings: (1) acute medical inpatient

units at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC), Boston, MA (2) elective surgical

inpatient services at the University of Florida Hospital (UF), Gainesville, FL and (3) nursing

home (long-term and post-acute care) at The New Jewish Home (TNJH), New York City, NY.

After completion of a gold standard rating of delirium severity (described below) a subsample of

46 cases was selected for the reliability sample; this sample size was selected based on the

inclusion of 7 assessors for which the à priori target number of cases to ensure statistically

stable results was estimated to be between 25 and 50 cases.13,14 Cases were selected

sequentially to ensure representation of both delirium positive and negative cases across all

study sites. Each of the 46 cases were rated by 7 clinical assessors resulting in 21 unique

pairwise comparisons; this resulted in 966 paired ratings across 46 participants (Supplemental

Figure 1). Missing ratings from one clinical assessor (for two participants) resulted in loss of 12

pairwise comparisons, yielding a final reliability sample of 954 paired ratings. Gold standard

assessment of delirium for the full BASIL II sample employed an expert panel adjudication

process, described previously.9,12 The gold standard diagnoses were used in the present study.

All study procedures, including informed consent, were approved by the BIDMC Institutional

Review Board with ceded review from UF and TNJH. All participants or their legal

representatives provided informed consent.

       Clinical Assessor Procedures. The Clinical Assessors (CAs) were clinicians and

experienced clinical researchers, including neuropsychologists (n=3), psychologist (n=1),

neurologist (n=1), mental health counselor (n=1), and clinical project manager (n = 1), who had

experience in the diagnosis of delirium and dementia. All CAs underwent centralized training

and standardization procedures, including didactic instruction, observed ratings, weekly case

review with the Site Leader, and bimonthly all-site check in meetings. The CAs conducted

structured interviews and ratings of participants as part of the BASIL II study.9

        For the reliability study, the seven CAs independently reviewed identical packets

containing a uniform set of information, including demographic data, in-person cognitive

performance (MoCA,11 Severe Impairment Battery [SIB-8]15), delirium features/severity (CAM,10

CAM-Severity [CAM-S]16), and informant-based tools (Family Assessment of Delirium [FAM-

CAM],17 Eight-item Informant Interview to Differentiate Aging and Dementia [AD8],18 Delirium

Rating Scale Revised 98 [DRS-R-98]19). Medical record documentation of medical comorbidities

and medications were also reviewed for each of the 46 cases. Delirium severity was rated using

a 0–10 scale, with anchors corresponding to absent (0), subsyndromal (1–3), mild-to-moderate

(4–6), and severe delirium (7–10). Clinical assessors were instructed to base their ratings solely

on the structured assessment materials provided in the case packets. The CA ratings for

delirium severity were compared, and initial agreement was considered achieved according to

the pre-specified threshold of 5 out of 7 clinical assessors agreeing on the presence or absence

of delirium, and no more than a 2-point range of disagreement among delirium severity scores

(scored on a 0-10 point scale). Following initial ratings, 13 cases achieved agreement, and 33

cases without consensus were discussed in consensus meetings. These meetings were

conducted in 11 sessions over 7 months, discussing 2-9 cases at each. See Supplemental

Figure 2 for assessment details and clinical assessor consensus process.

Statistical Analysis

       The analytic plan was pre-specified and implemented independently of the clinical

assessors. We used a multi-method approach to evaluate inter-rater reliability of the clinical

assessor delirium severity ratings.

       We evaluated inter-rater reliability for delirium severity scores (0-10 scale) using all

possible paired combinations of the 7 clinical assessors (n = 954 unique pairs). We examined

both Pearson correlation coefficients and weighted kappa coefficients (with quadratic weights)

to summarize the agreement among pairs and confirmed that both approaches yielded identical

results. We report these statistics as weighted kappa coefficients. These agreement statistics

were calculated before and after the structured consensus discussion to assess its effect on

reliability. For the binary classification of delirium (present/absent), we also calculated kappa (κ)

before and after consensus to assess diagnostic agreement. To address whether reliability

differed by the presence of dementia, we repeated weighted κ and percent agreement analyses

stratified by dementia status (present vs. absent, per gold-standard adjudication). We also

computed mean absolute differences between paired rater scores as a complementary

continuous index of disagreement on the 0-10 severity scale.

       We used Bland-Altman analysis20 to examine agreement between rater pairs across the

full range of severity (0-10). For each plot, we regressed the difference in scores (Rater A –

Rater B) on the pairwise mean score to test whether disagreement varied by severity level. A

slope that is approximately 0 indicates no systematic bias in agreement across the severity

spectrum. To reduce potential sampling bias and improve representativeness, we applied

analytic weights using entropy balancing21 to more closely match the subsample distribution to

the full BASIL II sample on site and delirium severity rating.

       All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.2).

===== PAGE 9 =====
RESULTS

       For the 954 paired ratings, delirium severity scores ranged from 0-9 (M = 2.4, SD = 2.6),

representing a wide range of clinical severity. Characteristics of the 46 cases comprising the

reliability sample appear in Supplemental Table 1.

       Figure 1 illustrates the high inter-rater agreement in delirium severity rating prior to and

following the consensus process. The weighted kappa was κ=0.84 (95% confidence interval, CI

0.82, 0.86) indicating strong consistency across assessors. Following the consensus meeting,

the weighted kappa increased to κ=0.93 (95% CI 0.92, 0.94). Table 1 shows a crosstab of

paired delirium presence (clinical assessor diagnosis) before (κ=0.77, 95% CI 0.73, 0.81) and

after consensus (κ=0.81, 95% CI 0.77, 0.85), both indicating excellent agreement.22

       Reliability was also examined separately for patients with and without dementia

(Supplemental Table 2). Inter-rater agreement was high and comparable across both strata.

The weighted kappa was κ=0.85 (95% CI 0.82, 0.88) in cases without dementia and κ= 0.84

(95% CI 0.81, 0.86) in cases with dementia prior to consensus, improving to κ=0.94 and κ=0.93

after consensus. Percent agreement reached 100 in both strata following consensus. Mean

absolute differences between paired rater scores were higher in dementia cases (1.20 pre, 0.77

post) than non-dementia cases (0.84 pre, 0.52 post) but remained within the prespecified 2-

point rater agreement window.

       To evaluate whether rating discrepancies varied as a function of delirium severity, Bland-

Altman analysis was conducted. There was no significant association between the mean

severity score and absolute disagreement across rater pairs before (linear regression

coefficient, b=0.03, 95% CI -0.13, 0.20) and after consensus (b=-0.01, 95% CI -0.13, 0.12)

suggesting that disagreement was not systematically greater at lower or higher ends of the

severity spectrum (Figure 2).

===== PAGE 10 =====
CONCLUSION

       This reliability study embedded in the larger BASIL II cohort shows that trained clinical

assessors can follow standardized protocols to produce highly reliable delirium severity ratings

across diverse care settings. Inter-rater agreement was strong prior to consensus discussion

and improved further after the structured consensus process. Importantly, agreement did not

vary across levels of delirium severity, supporting the consistency of this approach across the

full clinical spectrum. Agreement was also similarly high in cases with and without dementia,

supporting reliability of this approach in the population for whom delirium severity measurement

is most clinically needed and most challenging.

       While expert panel adjudication is considered the reference standard for delirium

diagnosis, it may not be feasible for large-scale studies, or those operating under resource

constraints. The BASIL II study is one of few studies to detail the expert panel adjudication

process.23 To enable rigorous validation of delirium diagnosis and the development of delirium

severity assessment tools, the study team employed a standardized approach to the initial

clinical assessment using a fixed protocol of measures administered by assessors with study-

specific training and weekly meetings across study-sites to discuss ratings. There is limited

evidence on the inter-rater reliability of structured clinical assessments of delirium conducted by

trained clinical assessors who vary in experience and qualifications.9 Our findings extend

previous work by demonstrating the reliability of delirium severity ratings when assessors are

trained and supported with standardized protocols, even in the absence of a consensus

process.

       The strength of this study is the use of structured summaries – including cognitive

testing (e.g., MoCA, SIB-8), standardized delirium assessments (e.g., CAM, CAM-S), informant

interviews – which allowed assessors to rate cases using uniform, high-quality data, and the

availability of expert reference standard diagnoses. This design minimized contextual bias and

===== PAGE 11 =====
may extrapolate to real-world scenarios in which site-based clinicians evaluate delirium based

on available assessment tools.

          A few limitations should be noted. First, the reliability study was conducted on

standardized summaries, which were condensed in-person assessments. A bedside clinical

impression was incorporated into the standardized materials for review but remained constant

across each rater. While this approach minimizes contextual biases, it may not fully reflect

clinical variation that may be appreciated at the bedside.24 Second, this relatively small reliability

sample (n=46 cases) is sufficient for inter-rater reliability estimation but not well-suited for

comparisons across study sites. For site and other levels of analysis, see Boockvar et al.’s

report of expert panel ratings using the full BASILII cohort. Third, assessors were part of a

research team with dedicated training and time, limiting generalizability to routine clinical care.

However, the high reliability achieved across independent raters from multiple disciplines

highlights the feasibility of this approach for research studies requiring graded measurement of

delirium severity.

    In conclusion, clinical assessors trained with standardized protocols demonstrated strong

inter-rater reliability of delirium severity ratings across varied settings and clinical profiles. These

findings support the use of structured clinical assessments to generate reproducible data in

geriatric care research and highlight the utility of rater training in multicenter studies. Delirium

severity is increasingly recognized as a robust predictor of clinical outcomes. Future work using

highly reliable, graded measurement of delirium has the potential to capture valuable symptom

information and positively impact clinical monitoring, pathophysiologic studies, and treatment

trials.
